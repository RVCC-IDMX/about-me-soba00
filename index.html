Hover Effects in Modern Web Design

Although simple and relatively easy to use, hover effects are one of those little flourishes that punch WAAAY above their weight class and do a lot of the aesthetic heavy lifting that makes websites feel more interactive and "alive." When combined with other effects like shadows, transforms, and transitions to animate effects, their use alone can radically reshape how a site feels. BUT that's getting a bit ahead of ourselves, first what actually are hover effects?

Well, MDN Web Docs states that the :hover pseudo-class is used to “apply styles when a user designates an element with a pointing device” (SOURCE: MDN Web Docs :hover CSS pseudo-class), which may sound complicated but to put it plainly, hover effects occur when you hover your mouse pointer over an element and CSS changes that element's attributes in response. We do this using :hover, a pseudo-class that gives the browser a set of styles to apply to a corresponding element ONLY when the cursor is over it. Simple right? Yeah, well, so are levers and y'know what Archimedes said. Despite their straightforwardness, due to the rather "haptically lacking" nature of websites, hover effects end up being one of the best (and only) ways we can provide users feedback as they navigate our site.

Personally, I used them a bunch in this very site! Go ahead and check out the Gallery again, as you hover your mouse over each picture, you'll see it expand and slightly glow. Pretty neat right? Not only does it add a nice flourish, but it also draws in the attention of visitors, with the expanding effect making the image easier to see and the glow effect visually emphasizing it from the background.

To do the actual effect, we just used the :hover class for the images in the Gallery section and paired it with the use of the "transform" rule to give the enlarging effect and a box shadow of a bright color to create a glow. I also used a hover effect in the Contact Me page to provide more of that "kinetic" feedback I mentioned earlier to the submission button. Though it may seem purely aesthetic, providing feedback to user interactions is ESSENTIAL for ease of navigation and lending a tactile response to user actions, and hover effects are amazing at this. To quote the Framer YouTube channel in their Mastering animations and interactions series: "Hover and press effects are small touches that make a big difference by giving visitors tangible feedback that makes it super clear if something is clickable in the first place and if they've clicked it successfully." (SOURCE: Using Hover and Press Effects in Framer (Animation Lesson 2)). Especially in the context of buttons and other interactables, making sure the user can find them and understand when they've successfully pressed them is critical to avoid mistakes or clunky UX, and hover effects are a great way to get started.

Ok so hover effects can be used as a launching off point to combine multiple effects and animations, they are one of the most simple and elegant ways to add feedback, AND they're easy to use? What can't they do, right?!

Well, not so fast because it turns out hover effects do still have their flaws. To start, it's incredibly easy to overdo them because they automatically react to cursor overlap. Having too many can "flashbang" your visitor with effects like popups, color changes, and glow effects, leading to an uncomfortable and distracting user experience. Hover effects also have some less straightforward drawbacks that I hadn't really even considered. When researching this topic for example, I stumbled upon an article titled, rather enticingly, “Why you should not use the hover event” and in it, Medium.com writer Joi Schünemann brings up the excellent point that you can't really “hover” on a touch screen. He points out: “The biggest issue is that there is no hover effect on touch screens. Since you are operating mobile devices with your fingers, there is no way to hover over certain elements.” (SOURCE: “Why you should not use the hover event”). This is important as it not only means a sizable percentage of users will never interact with an aspect of your site, it also means we can't solely rely on hover effects to do all the heavy lifting with feedback for users.

To end this post off here, for me, hover effects ended up being one of the simplest but most important parts of the site. They take barely any code to implement, but they change how the entire page feels as you interact with it.
